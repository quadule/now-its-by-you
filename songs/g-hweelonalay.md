# G. Hweelonalay

*A refrain in no language. Chanted 4/4 in phrygian, quarter-eighth-eighth
feet, a call-and-response refrain over the verses' flat second. Verses are
charms in dactyls, 10/7/10/7, xAxA.*

Refrain (say it: hwee-LO-na-LAY, a-YA-lu-MO, a-YA-lu-HOM):

> Hweelonalay, hweelonalay,
> ayalumo, ayaluhom;
> hweelonalaya, hweelonalay,
> yalumo, yalumo, hweelona, hom.

> Carry it low in the back of your throat,
> carry it all the day long;
> words are a coat, and the coat will wear through,
> sound is the bone of the song.

> Sing it three times with the words that you know,
> let them all fall to the earth;
> words are the fire, and the fire will go out,
> sound is the stone of the hearth.

> Give it to someone who hasn't your tongue,
> someone from over the hill;
> words will turn back at the edge of the map,
> sound goes wherever it will.

> Let the machine have it, let it go round,
> let it go round and about;
> words that it sings will be new every time,
> sound is the same coming out.

> Die if you must, with your mouth left ajar,
> open a window as well;
> nothing goes into the ground but the words,
> nobody buries a spell.

> Nobody made it, it came with the breath,
> everyone's breathing the same;
> words have their makers, and makers have graves,
> sound never answered a name.

## Note

**The idea.** The words are for forgetting; the sound is for keeping. The
refrain is built only from the noises a mouth makes when it has no words: a
breathy whistle (*hwee*), a lull (*lo-na-lay*, the syllables of every
lullaby), a call (*ya*), a baby's *ma* and *mo*, and a hum to close (*hom*).
Those sounds are in every mouth before any language, so the refrain belongs to
every border equally and there is nothing in it to translate or to own. The
verses, in plain English, are the rules for carrying such a thing: charms in
the imperative, like the Lyke-Wake Dirge's instructions to the dead. Each one
ends by setting words, which perish (a coat, a fire, a map, a grave), against
sound, which stays (bone, hearthstone, a spell nobody can bury).

**What's weird.** It is a song that tells you to forget its own words, and it
means it: it says the verses you are hearing are a coat that will wear through,
and that the sounds are the bone. That is literally how Songbird works, since
the verses are rewritten every generation and the refrain never is, so the
song's ritual and the program's mechanism are the same thing. The refrain is
sung to a whistle-and-hum call that leaps to the high tonic and leans on the
phrygian flat second, then tumbles by leaps onto the hum; the response line is
left hanging on the flat second and only resolves at the end of the refrain.
The instrumental break is the verse tune with no words at all, a verse whose
words have already fallen away.

**What the LLM refills.** Lines 1 and 2 of each verse are a new rule for
handling the song (carry it, sing it, give it, let it, die with it), one
imperative or one condition. Lines 3 and 4 are a fixed frame, "words [are a
perishable thing and how it perishes] / sound [is the thing that stays]",
with line 4 rhyming line 2. Stress is fixed by the tune: S w w S w w S w w S
over S w w S w w S. The refrain is fixed forever and the template forbids
glossing it: a verse that says what the sounds mean is a bad fill.

**Death and passing on.** Passing on is the third verse: give it to someone
who hasn't your language, because the refrain crosses where words turn back,
and a hum or whistle of it is the whole thing. The fourth lets a machine have
it, on the machine's real terms: new words every time, the same sound coming
out. Death is the fifth: die with your mouth ajar and a window open (the old
custom for letting a soul out), and only the words go into the ground. The
last verse answers who made it: nobody, it came with the breath; makers have
graves, and the sound never answered a name.
