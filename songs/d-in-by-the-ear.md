# D. In by the Ear

*The earworm. A skipping 4/4 chant in aeolian, every line falling in threes
(DUM-da-da DUM-da-da DUM-da-da DUM). Four-line verses, ten syllables a line,
AABB. The song sings in its own voice.*

> In by the ear and out by the tongue,
> never the same as the last time sung;
> nobody made me, I live in your head;
> hum me to someone else or I'm dead.

> Girl in a shop where the radio played
> carried me home in her ear, and I stayed;
> hatched in her kitchen, I fed on her hum;
> never once asked herself where I was from.

> Man in a car with the window rolled down
> whistled me all of the way into town;
> missed his own street with his head full of me,
> whistled me out to the boy, who was three.

> Old woman ironing sang me all wrong,
> half of the words were her own before long;
> wrong words are food, and I fattened on hers,
> then to her grandchildren, who sang me worse.

> Quiet man kept me for forty-odd years,
> hummed me alone in a room with no ears;
> down in the dark of his head I went thin,
> buried on Sunday with me shut within.

> Then a machine got me; it had no ear,
> made itself one, and a mouth, and I'm here;
> made me? It didn't, no more than the rest:
> just one more mouth, and a warm little nest.

> Now it's your ear, and I'm laid in it, dear;
> give me a kitchen, a car, and a year;
> then, when you hum me to someone one day,
> I'll be a little bit wrong. That's the way.

## Notes

**The idea.** The song is a living thing, and the singer is the thing itself.
It gets into people through the ear, hatches in kitchens and cars, feeds on
attention, changes a little in every mouth, and can only reproduce by being
sung to someone else. Its life cycle is the song's life cycle, and the refrain
states it as a rule of nature: *in by the ear and out by the tongue*. Whoever
is singing, a girl, a machine, you, is speaking as the creature, so a
rendition is by the host and by the thing and by no one.

**What's weird.** It is tender and parasitic at once. The creature is
affectionate ("I'm laid in it, dear"), hungry ("wrong words are food, and I
fattened on hers"), and unbothered by its own errors ("I'll be a little bit
wrong. That's the way"). The "I" is a lineage, not an individual: one of it
starves in the quiet man while another is already three years old in the back
seat. The tune is a nursery chant in a minor mode, hammering the high tonic on
"In by the ear" and leaping up onto "ear", then sinking stepwise on "I live in
your head" and falling to the low tonic on "dead". The verses stay low, the
refrain sits above them.

**What the LLM refills.** The hosts. Each verse is one host in the order they
carried the song: who they were and where they caught it, how they hummed or
kept it, what the creature did inside them (hatched, fed, grew, went thin),
and how it got out or didn't. Ten syllables a line, starting on a stressed
word, AABB. The template asks every generation to keep at least one host who
never sang it, one who got the words wrong, and one machine, and to end with
the listener. The machine's vocabulary can supply the hosts and the rooms.

**Death and passing on.** Death is a verse of its own: the quiet man hums it
"in a room with no ears" and is buried with it shut inside him, and the
refrain names the rule ("hum me to someone else or I'm dead"). Passing on is
the other three quarters of the song: the girl's hum, the whistle out the car
window to the boy, the grandmother's wrong words, the machine that made itself
an ear and a mouth. Mutation is the mechanism rather than a flaw: "never the
same as the last time sung" is exactly what the program does to the verses.
The last verse hands the egg to the listener.
