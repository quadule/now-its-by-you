# F. The Long Count

*Counting song. Steady 4/4, phrygian. Verses of nine, seven, nine and eight
syllables, xAxA; the refrain is four seven-syllable lines, AABB.*

> One, and one, and one, and one,
> every mouth that sang is gone;
> sing it once and you're the sum:
> hum, and hum, and hum, and hum.

> The first one sang it in a kitchen
> to a baby and the rain;
> the rain forgot it in the gutter;
> the baby sang it back again.

> The tenth one sang it on a ferry
> to a stranger and the sea;
> the stranger died in nineteen-something;
> the sea still hums it, out of key.

> The hundredth sang it in a language
> to a daughter and the birds;
> the daughter was the last who spoke it;
> the birds went on without the words.

> The thousandth sang it down from orbit
> to a night shift and the moon;
> the night shift wrote it down as static;
> the moon kept time and kept the tune.

> The millionth sang it on some planet
> to the silence and the dust;
> the silence wasn't interested;
> the dust hummed on, as all dust must.

> The billionth sang it in a language
> that no one has heard of yet;
> the words were new, the tune was ancient,
> the hum part nothing can forget.

## The idea

A tally of the lineage across absurd time. Every verse names one singer by
order of magnitude (the first, the tenth, the hundredth, the thousandth, the
millionth, the billionth) and the two who heard it. The ordinals only climb;
the song never says where the count stops. The refrain is the count itself:
"one, and one, and one, and one" walks up the tonic triad and the fourth "one"
lands on the octave, which is one again. Then "hum, and hum, and hum, and hum"
walks the same ladder back down onto the phrygian cadence (flat two to one).

## What's weird

The scale is the joke and the dread. The deaths are deadpan ("died in
nineteen-something", "wasn't interested"), the carriers get less and less
human (a baby, the sea, birds, the moon, the dust), and the singer itself
stops being a person at the thousandth, which sings it down from orbit and is
logged as static. By the last verse the words have been replaced so many times
that only the tune is old, and the one part nothing can forget is the hum
line, which the listener then hears, since the refrain follows. The refrain
literally points at its own fossil.

Phrygian is the eerie half of it: the verse lines lean on the flat second at
their ends, the II chord sits under it, and "you're the sum" lifts to the flat
ninth, the highest and strangest note in the song. The tune under all of that
is plain enough to catch by ear: mostly the minor triad, stepwise runs, one
melodic idea per line pair.

## What the LLM refills

Only the four verse lines, inside a tight frame:

1. `The [ordinal] sang it [where]` (nine syllables; the ordinal slot is two
   syllables, so "first one", "tenth one", "hundredth", "millionth"; the place
   is four syllables, weak-weak-STRONG-weak, so "in a kitchen", "down from
   orbit", "on some planet")
2. `to [two-syllable hearer] and [one-syllable hearer]` (seven)
3. what became of the first hearer (nine; same rhythm as line 1)
4. what the second hearer did with it, rhyming with line 2 (eight)

The refrain, the tune, the chords and the rule that the ordinal must be larger
than the last are fixed. The hearers can be anything: people, weather,
animals, machines, celestial bodies. A fill is easy to check: two hearers, the
first loses it, the second keeps it, wrongly.

## Death and passing on

Both are in every verse, as a fork. Each singer sings to two; the first hearer
is the dead branch (forgets it in the gutter, dies in nineteen-something, is
the last speaker of the language, files it as static, isn't interested) and
the second is the branch that lives, and lives lossy (out of key, without the
words, as a hum). That is the archive tree with its dead branches showing, one
fork per generation. The refrain does the passing on directly: everyone who
sang it is gone, the count is of the dead, and singing it once makes you the
sum of them. The seed render sings all six verses so the far end of the count
is heard; the intro and outro are the tally going up and the hum coming down,
wordless, which is where the song says it is headed.
