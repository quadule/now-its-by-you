Now It's by You

About the song

AI-generated music has become synonymous with the fake artists and prompt-to-song outputs that have flooded music streaming services in recent years, but its history is deeper and lesser known. For the 2026 AI Song Contest, I wanted to explore the key issues of authorship and compensation by looking to two traditions that challenge our conventional notions of them: folk music and open source software.

An open source folk song

Folk songs change as they travel and lose their authors along the way. Each singer learns a song from someone else, changes a line or a turn of the melody, and passes it on. Open source software travels in a similar way: anyone can take the code, change it and share the result. Its history records every change and who made it, but its authors are rarely paid for it.

Now It's by You is my attempt at a song that works like both. The program that sings it travels inside every copy, so anyone who has the song can change it. Each version records the one it came from, and singers from up to six earlier versions join in for harmony during the bridge. There, the melody also quotes “Daisy Bell”, the first song a computer sang, in 1961, so the song's lineage includes machines as well as people.

This song is built upon the creative works of countless uncredited artists before me, including the unknown singers, writers, and programmers whose work trained the AI models I used. So I've dedicated it to the public domain: no one can quite say who made it, and no one should own it.

How it was made

The idea came to me a few days before the contest deadline: a folk song changing as it passes between computers as well as people, with authorship blurred between them. I could only realize this vision in its totality by treating AI agents as full creative collaborators and taking the role of part director and part editor.

I primarily used Claude Code and Opus 5.5 to develop a Python program that synthesizes every instrument and voice from a template we wrote together in the style of a ballad: a tune, a refrain, a bank of verses, and the rules for how they change from one version to the next.

The voice itself was the most challenging, as I wanted it to sound both human and synthetic at the same time. It started as a classic formant synthesizer, then became a small learned model trained on singing from other open voice models, and finally was fine-tuned on the takes of eight volunteers who sang the song through a karaoke-style website (with all recordings deleted shortly after).
